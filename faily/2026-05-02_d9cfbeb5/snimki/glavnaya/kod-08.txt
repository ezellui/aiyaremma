A vertical philosophical poster in the style of post-war French intellectual 
print (think mid-century Galilée, Seuil, or Verdier book covers) crossed 
with quiet anime sentai illustration. Title at the top in clean serif 
capitals: "SAILOR VISAGE", with subtitle below: "ETHICS AS FIRST 
PHILOSOPHY". Beneath the subtitle, a smaller French motto: "L'ÉTHIQUE 
COMME PHILOSOPHIE PREMIÈRE".

CRITICAL STRUCTURE: this poster is asymmetric in a way none of the previous 
have been. The Sailor figure does NOT stand in the center. She stands in 
the LOWER-LEFT third of the central scene, looking UPWARD AND RIGHTWARD 
toward a TALLER FIGURE who occupies the upper-right zone — the OTHER, 
l'Autrui. Their relation is asymmetric by HEIGHT: the Other is taller, 
positioned higher in the frame, the Sailor lower. This is "la hauteur du 
visage" — the height of the face, an ethical not a physical elevation.

Background setting: a quiet undefined urban-architectural space at dusk or 
just before dawn — neither cityscape nor wilderness, more like the inner 
courtyard of an old building, with simple stone walls, a single doorway 
half-visible, a low stone bench, a sense of a threshold (le seuil) rather 
than an enclosed room or open landscape. The light is a SOFT TWILIGHT 
GLOW, neither day nor night. Palette: warm sand, dusty ochre, weathered 
stone grey, with a single deep DARK BLUE accent (the color of dusk and of 
a Jewish prayer shawl, taleth) — but the blue is restrained, used only as 
an accent on the Sailor's shawl and at the horizon.

THE SAILOR (lower-left): a young woman in her late twenties, slender, 
modest. She wears an austere sailor-senshi-style uniform adapted for 
sobriety: a plain cream linen blouse with a simple sailor collar trimmed 
with deep navy blue (NOT classical white-and-blue), a knee-length skirt in 
soft beige wool (longer than typical senshi skirts — modest, ankle-length 
NOT permitted but mid-calf or just below the knee), simple low brown 
leather shoes, a deep navy blue scarf or shawl draped over her left 
shoulder (an understated allusion to a prayer shawl, taleth). NO ornaments, 
NO gold, NO jewelry. Hair dark, simply tied back, with a few strands loose.

Tiara: a SINGLE PLAIN THIN SILVER BAND across the brow, NO gem, NO emblem, 
almost invisible.

A simple medallion at the chest, NO eye and NO sun and NO geometric figure: 
a plain silver disc engraved in tiny Hebrew letters with the word "הִנֵּנִי" 
(hineni — "here I am"). Below the disc, in smaller serif Latin letters: 
"ME VOICI".

CRITICAL POSE AND HANDS: she stands turned three-quarters to her right, 
upper body slightly inclined toward the Other. Both her hands are open, 
palms up, held forward at chest level — completely empty, BARE, 
defenseless. In her right palm, ONE SMALL PIECE OF SIMPLE BREAD (a torn 
piece of plain bread, no ornament). This is the ethical primal gesture for 
Levinas: "to give the bread from one's mouth". Her left palm is empty, 
also offered. The hands are held as in offering, not as in begging. Her 
expression: serious, attentive, NOT smiling, NOT severe — quietly present, 
attending to the Other.

Her gaze: directed UPWARD AND RIGHTWARD, into the FACE of the Other. NOT 
at the viewer, NOT into distance, NOT at her own hands. This is the eighth 
and final position of gaze in the series: the gaze upward into the face 
of the Other.

THE OTHER (upper-right): the most delicate part of the poster. The Other 
is a TALLER FIGURE, positioned in the upper-right third, partially turned 
in three-quarters profile so that we, the viewers, can see them from 
slightly behind — we see the back of the Other's shoulder and the side of 
the Other's head turned toward the Sailor. The Other's CLOTHING is simple, 
shabby, not specifically gendered or aged: a long worn coat in muted dark 
tones, simple shoes, a sense of poverty and dignity together. The Other 
could be old or young, male or female — DELIBERATELY ambiguous, because 
the Other in Levinas is anyone whose face confronts me with responsibility.

CRITICALLY: the Other's FACE is shown in profile, but its features are 
SOFTLY BLURRED, almost luminous, lacking sharp definition. NOT a portrait, 
NOT a specific person — instead, a face rendered as PRESENCE rather than 
image. Light seems to emanate from the contour of the face itself, 
slightly glowing, while the features within are simplified, soft, 
unindividualized. This is "le visage" — the face that resists being made 
into an image. 

The Other's HEAD is slightly inclined toward the Sailor, gaze meeting hers. 
We can see ONE EYE of the Other in profile, gentle and present. One of 
the Other's hands is visible at their side, also empty, palm half-open — 
mirroring the Sailor's offered hands but at a different height. Between 
their two extended hands, in the air between Sailor and Other, soft 
volumetric light catches dust motes — a charged empty space of ethical 
relation.

ABOVE the entire scene, running across the FULL WIDTH of the upper edge of 
the poster: a NARROW DARK BAND, perhaps 8-10% of the poster's height, 
rendered in bleak greys and blacks, suggesting (without depicting 
explicitly) a horizon of barbed wire, distant figures in striped uniforms, 
empty barrack silhouettes — the catastrophe of the 20th century, the Shoah, 
visible only as a horizon. NO swastikas, NO Nazi imagery, NO graphic 
violence. Just a band of darkness with the single white serif inscription: 
"APRÈS — APRÈS L'ONTOLOGIE". This dark band is visually OPPRESSIVE but 
NARROW, and the Sailor in the main scene below DOES NOT LOOK AT IT — she 
looks at the face of the Other. This is the structural point: ethics 
responds to horror by turning to the face, not by representing horror.

LEFT BORDER COLUMN — six small ornate cartouches with key Levinasian 
concepts:
- "LE VISAGE" — a luminous outline of a face without features, only a 
  glowing contour suggesting presence.
- "L'HOSPITALITÉ" — an open door at twilight with warm light from inside, 
  a simple table with two place settings visible.
- "L'INSOMNIE" — a figure lying awake in the dark, eyes open, a single 
  small lamp burning. Caption beneath: "Il y a".
- "LE TIERS" — three figures in conversation: two facing each other and a 
  third standing behind them, the third opening the question of justice.
- "LA TRACE" — not a footprint but a faint trail of light receding, the 
  trace of someone (or Someone) who has already passed.
- "LA CARESSE" — two hands almost touching, fingertips approaching but 
  NOT meeting, the gesture of approach without grasp.

RIGHT BORDER COLUMN — six small ornate cartouches with further concepts:
- "L'AUTRE" — a single open eye, large, in profile, looking out of the 
  frame.
- "LA RESPONSABILITÉ" — one hand reaching out to support another hand 
  that is falling.
- "LA SUBSTITUTION" — two figures merging at the shoulder, one stepping 
  into the place of the other.
- "L'OTAGE" — wrists bound by a soft golden cord, but the cord extends 
  outward to another figure rather than confining; bondage as 
  responsibility, not imprisonment.
- "AUTREMENT QU'ÊTRE" — the title of his late masterwork; a glowing word 
  written on parchment, half-erased, half-rewritten.
- "TOTALITÉ ET INFINI" — the title of his early masterwork; a closed 
  perfect circle next to an open spiral extending beyond the frame.

ANTAGONISTIC ELEMENT — a single cartouche placed at the lower-center 
between the two biographical scenes:
- "LA TOTALITÉ" — a perfectly enclosed circle containing many small 
  identical figures, all the same, none distinct; with a thin line crossing 
  the circle indicating its limit. Caption beneath: "L'ontologie comme 
  philosophie de la puissance". This is Levinas's central philosophical 
  antagonist: the totalizing system that absorbs the Other into the Same.

BOTTOM CORNERS — two biographical scenes in plain rectangular frames:

- Bottom left, labeled "KOVNO, LITUANIE, années 1920": a quiet interior 
  scene of a Lithuanian Jewish bookseller's home — a young boy of about 
  ten reading at a table by candlelight, an open Hebrew book before him, 
  his father (a bookseller) at a bookshelf in the background, his mother 
  half-visible in a doorway. Warm but austere domestic light. A tallit on 
  a chair in the background. This is the world that was destroyed.

- Bottom right, labeled "STALAG XI-B, 1940–1945": a starker scene — wooden 
  barracks of a German prisoner-of-war camp at dusk, a man in worn French 
  military uniform sitting at a small table near the window, writing in a 
  small notebook by weak light. He is alone except for ONE SMALL DOG 
  nearby — a striped dog wagging its tail, looking at him with simple 
  recognition (this is "Bobby, the only Kantian in Nazi Germany"). The 
  notebook on the table is the future "De l'existence à l'existant".

These two corner scenes form a pair: WORLD that was destroyed and CAMP 
where philosophy survived. Between them, the destroyed family and the 
preserving notebook.

Bottom of the poster, in clean serif capitals: "ME VOICI." Below it in 
smaller italic French: "Après vous, Monsieur." Bottommost banner across 
the full width, in serif small caps: "L'AUTRE EST PLUS HAUT QUE MOI — ET 
C'EST POURQUOI JE SUIS RESPONSABLE."

Style: post-war French intellectual book cover aesthetic with restrained 
typography, photorealistic but quiet anime figure design, ASYMMETRIC 
composition with the Sailor lower-left and the Other upper-right, palette 
of warm sand, weathered stone, and accents of deep dusk blue, soft 
twilight lighting, vertical poster format, MUCH NEGATIVE SPACE between 
the two figures (the empty zone of ethical relation), the dark horizon 
band at the top, museum print quality, atmosphere of quiet ethical 
gravity and twilight presence.
