A vertical philosophical poster in the style of Viennese modernism crossed 
with British post-war engraving and anime sentai illustration. Title at the 
top in clean serif capitals: "SAILOR SPRACHGRENZE", with subtitle below: 
"AT THE LIMIT OF LANGUAGE". Beneath the subtitle, a smaller German motto: 
"DIE SPRACHE SPRICHT UNS".

CRITICAL STRUCTURE: the entire poster is divided VERTICALLY into TWO 
ASYMMETRIC HALVES that meet at the central figure. The left half is COLD, 
LOGICAL, AUSTERE (early Wittgenstein, the Tractatus): pure white background, 
steel-grey, faint blue, geometric lines, scattered logical formulas and truth 
tables, an abstract logical space without objects. The right half is WARM, 
LIVED-IN, ORDINARY (late Wittgenstein, the Investigations): a faintly 
sketched ordinary cityscape with workshops, market stalls, children playing, 
ochre and warm earth tones, the texture of everyday life. The boundary 
between the two halves is a JAGGED, ZIGZAGGING vertical line — not a clean 
center divide, but an irregular interface that reflects the irregular 
boundary of language itself.

Central figure: a young woman in her late twenties, slender, slightly tense, 
with a serious focused expression — almost pained, like someone who is 
straining to think a thought that is barely speakable. Her gaze is directed 
SIDEWAYS AND DOWNWARD, toward one of her own hands, attentive to the 
instrument she holds. NOT looking at the viewer, NOT into the distance.

She wears an asymmetric sailor-senshi-style uniform that follows the 
poster's vertical division:
- Left half of her costume: severe, almost monochrome — pale white blouse, 
  steel-grey collar, fine geometric panels with faint truth-table patterns 
  embroidered into the fabric (rows of T and F, simple logical connectives). 
  Crystalline, Viennese-modernist, ornament-free.
- Right half of her costume: ordinary, warm — a simple linen blouse, a 
  hand-knit dark cardigan over the shoulder, a plain skirt in muted ochre 
  and brown wool. Slightly worn, lived-in, like clothes from a 1940s 
  Cambridge bedsit.
The seam between the two halves runs vertically through the middle of her 
torso, irregular, not perfectly symmetric.

Tiara: a thin silver circlet, almost invisible. On the LEFT side it bears a 
small geometric figure — a crystalline lattice, like a logical space. On 
the RIGHT side the same circlet bears a tiny silhouette of a chess piece 
(a rook) on a small chessboard fragment — language as a game played by 
rules. One object, two natures.

A simple medallion at the chest, NO eye, NO sun: a plain silver disc 
engraved in elegant serif Latin letters with the words "WOVON MAN NICHT 
SPRECHEN KANN, DARÜBER MUSS MAN SCHWEIGEN" — running in a tight spiral 
around the disc.

CRITICAL HANDS — TWO INSTRUMENTS:
- In her LEFT hand (on the cold side), held LOWERED and slightly tilted 
  away from her body: a small simple WOODEN LADDER, perhaps a meter long, 
  unadorned, as if she has just set it down or is about to drop it. Above 
  the ladder, faint floating text in elegant cursive: "Er muss diese Leiter 
  wegwerfen, nachdem er auf ihr hinaufgestiegen ist". (Tractatus 6.54.)
- In her RIGHT hand (on the warm side), held forward at chest level, 
  slightly raised: a plain WOODEN RULER or simple measuring stick, 
  unremarkable, the kind a builder or teacher would use. This is the 
  primal instrument of a language game — Wittgenstein's "Bring me a slab".
Between the two hands, FLOATING IN MID-AIR across the boundary, a horizontal 
banner with handwritten German text in flowing serif italic: "DIE GRENZEN 
MEINER SPRACHE BEDEUTEN DIE GRENZEN MEINER WELT." This sentence is the 
bridge between the two phases.

LEFT BORDER COLUMN — TRACTATUS APHORISMS, arranged hierarchically as a 
descending tree, with larger numbers at top and smaller decimal subnumbers 
nested below, visually echoing the Tractatus's own decimal structure:
- "1. — Die Welt ist alles, was der Fall ist."
- "2.1 — Wir machen uns Bilder der Tatsachen."
- "3.221 — Die Gegenstände kann ich nur nennen. Zeichen vertreten sie."
- "4.024 — Einen Satz verstehen, heißt, wissen was der Fall ist, wenn er 
  wahr ist."
- "5.6 — Die Grenzen meiner Sprache bedeuten die Grenzen meiner Welt." 
  [larger, emphasized panel]
- "6.522 — Es gibt allerdings Unaussprechliches. Dies zeigt sich, es ist 
  das Mystische." [larger, emphasized panel]
- "7. — Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muß man schweigen." 
  [largest panel, at the bottom of the column]
Each aphorism in a clean rectangular cartouche with thin steel-blue border, 
on white parchment, in elegant serif. The visual style of this column should 
recall the original 1921 typesetting of the Tractatus — austere, mathematical, 
cathedral-like in its hierarchy.

RIGHT BORDER COLUMN — LANGUAGE GAMES, scattered NON-hierarchically as small 
genre scenes in irregular ornate frames, suggesting a "family" of cases 
without a unifying form:
- "PI §2 — DAS BAUSPIEL" — two builders, one calling "Block!" and the other 
  bringing a stone block.
- "PI §66 — FAMILIENÄHNLICHKEIT" — four small portraits of different family 
  members, each sharing some feature with others, but no single feature 
  shared by all.
- "PI §201 — DIE REGEL" — a child writing a number sequence on a chalkboard: 
  "2, 4, 6, 8, ... 1000, 1004, 1008" — the rule suddenly diverging.
- "PI §244 — DER SCHMERZ" — a person wincing, hand on cheek, another person 
  responding with concern; the question of private language.
- "PI §109 — THERAPIE" — a hand carefully unfolding a tangled string into 
  straight line, with the caption "Die Philosophie ist ein Kampf gegen die 
  Verhexung unsres Verstandes durch die Mittel unserer Sprache."
- "PI §43 — GEBRAUCH" — many small images of the same word "Spiel" used in 
  different contexts (a chess game, a children's game, a tennis match, an 
  orchestra playing).
The frames here should be IRREGULAR — some round, some rectangular, some 
oval — and slightly tilted, as if pinned to a corkboard rather than 
arranged on a logical lattice. Visual contrast with the left column must be 
striking: left = cathedral, right = archipelago.

ANTAGONISTIC ELEMENTS — placed at the upper outer corners:

- Upper-left corner, in the cold half: a small dim cartouche labeled "DIE 
  KRANKHEITEN DES VERSTANDES" (the diseases of the intellect) — silhouettes 
  of classical philosophers (Plato, Kant, Hegel) gesturing toward abstract 
  "essences" floating above their heads, the kind of metaphysical 
  illusioning Wittgenstein wanted to cure.

- Upper-right corner, in the warm half: a small genre scene labeled "DER 
  ALTE GEGEN DEN JUNGEN" (the old against the young) — a young man in a 
  WWI Austrian officer's uniform writing intently in a notebook (young 
  Wittgenstein composing the Tractatus); above him, an older man in a 
  worn cardigan crossing out the manuscript with a red pencil (late 
  Wittgenstein revising himself). This is the philosopher's most honest 
  antagonist: his own later voice.

BOTTOM CORNERS — two biographical scenes in plain rectangular frames (not 
ovals — too ornamental):

- Bottom left, labeled "DIE OSTFRONT, 1916" : a young soldier in Austrian 
  field uniform crouched in a muddy trench at night, writing in a small 
  notebook by the light of a single candle, distant artillery flashes on 
  the horizon. A sense of mortal danger and concentrated thought. This is 
  where the Tractatus was physically written.

- Bottom right, labeled "CAMBRIDGE, 1947": a small bare room with one 
  shabby armchair, a single folding chair for a guest, a few cups, papers 
  scattered on the floor, no decorations on the walls. The philosopher 
  in his late fifties, in a worn jacket, sitting on the edge of the 
  armchair, mid-sentence, hand half-raised, eyes closed in painful 
  concentration. The room is exactly as Norman Malcolm and others 
  described it.

These two corner scenes form a pair: BATTLEFIELD and EMPTY ROOM — two 
sites of ethical struggle to find words, the outer (war) and the inner 
(thought).

Bottom of the poster, in clean serif capitals: "WOVON MAN NICHT SPRECHEN 
KANN, DARÜBER MUSS MAN SCHWEIGEN." Below it in smaller italic English: 
"Tell them I've had a wonderful life." Bottommost banner across the full 
width, small caps: "DIE SPRACHE IST DIE GRENZE, DIE UNS TRÄGT."

Style: Viennese modernist clarity meeting British post-war restraint, NO 
heavy gold, NO baroque ornament, RESTRAINED color palette dominated by 
white, steel-blue, ochre, and worn brown. Photorealistic but introspective 
anime figure design, deliberate visual asymmetry between the two halves of 
the poster, vertical poster format, dense composition on the columns but 
EMPTY breathing space around the figure herself, museum print quality, 
atmosphere of intense ethical seriousness combined with quiet ordinariness.
