A vertical baroque philosophical poster in the same style as a 17th–18th-century 
frontispiece crossed with anime sentai illustration. Title at the top in 
ornate serif capitals: "SAILOR MONADE", with subtitle below: "MIRROR OF THE 
UNIVERSE". Beneath the subtitle, a smaller Latin motto: "CUM DEUS CALCULAT, 
FIT MUNDUS".

Central figure: a young woman in a slight baroque contrapposto turn, caught 
mid-unfolding, with a serene faintly smiling expression. Her costume and skin 
are made of polished mother-of-pearl and opal — a single continuous iridescent 
surface, softly glowing, shifting through pale rainbow tones (rose, mint, 
lavender, gold) with every implied movement. The dominant secondary color is 
deep baroque gold in the ornamentation. The whole figure must read as ONE 
seamless surface — no visible cuts, no seams, no joints — yet endlessly 
reflective.

CRITICAL DESIGN: the dress is rendered as INFINITE FOLDS (in the manner of 
Bernini's drapery taken to fractal extremity). Each large fold contains 
smaller folds, which contain smaller folds still, all the way down to the 
limit of resolution. The silhouette is simple from a distance, infinitely 
intricate up close — the baroque pli as ontological principle.

In the highlights and reflective surfaces of her dress, dozens of TINY 
ALTERNATE VERSIONS of herself are visible in different poses and slightly 
different costumes, like reflections of possible worlds — each blink of the 
fabric shows another monad's perspective. The actual central figure is the 
"actualized world"; the reflected versions are "possible worlds" not chosen.

Tiara: a perfect crystal sphere mounted on an elegant golden circlet; inside 
the sphere, many smaller crystal spheres are visible, each containing a tiny 
luminous world of its own — galaxies, cities, gardens, faces. One of the 
inner spheres glows brighter than the others, marking the actual world.

In her hands: TWO ornate baroque pocket watches connected by a fine golden 
chain — but the chain does NOT connect the mechanisms, it merely hangs 
between them. Both watches show the EXACT same time, their hands in perfect 
synchrony. This is preestablished harmony made visible. Tiny engraving on 
the watch faces in French: "harmonie préétablie".

Costume details: a high baroque collar with intricate lace patterning that 
itself fractally repeats, an elaborate stomacher with a central medallion 
showing a single closed eye on a windowless tower (the monad without 
windows), elegant cuffs, a baroque half-cape of iridescent fabric.

Border composition (replacing historical panels): instead of a temporal 
sequence of epochs, the entire frame is a GRID OF POSSIBLE WORLDS — twelve 
to sixteen small ornate medallions arranged around the central figure, each 
containing a tiny scene from a different possible world. Examples:
- "MUNDUS I" — Caesar does not cross the Rubicon; the Roman Senate intact.
- "MUNDUS II" — Adam does not fall; a luminous garden untouched.
- "MUNDUS III" — Leibniz at the court of Peter the Great in Moscow.
- "MUNDUS IV" — a city built on perfect geometric harmony, with binary 
  numerals carved on its gates (0 and 1).
- "MUNDUS V" — two clocks hanging in a void, ticking in perfect unison.
- "MUNDUS VI" — an alchemist's library where all books are open simultaneously.
- "MUNDUS VII" — characters of the I Ching arranged as binary code, glowing.
- "MUNDUS VIII" — a calculating engine of brass gears producing infinite 
  combinations of letters ("calculus ratiocinator").
- And several more, each labeled "MUNDUS [Roman numeral]".
ONE of the medallions, positioned at top center directly above the figure, 
is haloed with a brighter golden glow and labeled "MUNDUS OPTIMUS — HIC ET 
NUNC" — this is our world, the best of all possible worlds.

Bottom corners — two small historical scenes in oval frames:
- Bottom left: "HANNOVER" — an elegant baroque library at night, a man in 
  full periwig writing by candlelight, surrounded by clocks, mathematical 
  diagrams, and Chinese hexagrams on a side table.
- Bottom right: "DER KALKÜL" — a close-up of a hand drafting the symbols of 
  infinitesimal calculus (∫, dx, dy) and binary digits (0, 1) on parchment; 
  brass calculating wheels visible in the background.

Bottom of the poster, in elegant capitals: "CUM DEUS CALCULAT, FIT MUNDUS". 
Below it in smaller italic French: "Pourquoi y a-t-il quelque chose plutôt 
que rien?" Bottommost banner across the full width, small caps: "LA MONADE 
N'A POINT DE FENÊTRES — ET POURTANT ELLE EST LE MIROIR DE L'UNIVERS".

Style: ornate baroque illustration with heavy gold filigree, photorealistic 
anime figure design, late-baroque Dutch/German engraving aesthetic, mother-
of-pearl and opal as dominant material, vertical poster format, extremely 
detailed fractal drapery, dense composition, museum print quality.
